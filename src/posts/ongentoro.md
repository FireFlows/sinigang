## Title

音源吐露 (*ongentoro*) is a title pun, which makes it difficult to translate. [vgperson calls it "Synth Expression" on her website](https://vgperson.com/vocalhighlights.php?m=2026-03). "Sound Source Revealed" is what the YouTube video claims it's called in English, but I don't know about that one…

Avogado6 loves title puns (hey, I do, too; I'm a very punny guy *cue laugh track*). Their demo song for [Calogado](https://vocadb.net/Ar/144786)'s first voicebank, [OREMO UTAU](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tFcd1Fv7jLM), has a title pun. (OREMO is a program used to record UTAU voicebanks, but "OREMO UTAU" in Japanese — as 俺も歌う — means "I'll sing, too.")

A song they made with flower, [へる (*heru*)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3qjpOmdZ5uE), is also a title pun (*heru* means to diminish but could also be "hell." Both meanings are used in the song).

[オンゲントロ](https://dic.nicovideo.jp/a/%E3%82%AA%E3%83%B3%E3%82%B2%E3%83%B3%E3%83%88%E3%83%AD) (*ongentoro* spelled in katakana; link is region-blocked) is a term that mainly prevails in the Japanese vocalsynth community. It's a youkai that utters the spell "don't you want to record a voicebank, too?" (お前も音源を録らないか？, *omae mo **ongen** o **tora**nai ka?*)

録る (toru, to record) is [inflected](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/inflection#English) in both *ongen**toro*** and ***toranai***, but it's still the same base verb. Specifically: *toro* is in the imperative (commanding you do something) and *toranai* in the negative (but before *ka*, it's used to be like "won't you?").

Basically, the name of the youkai is "record a voicebank!"

The title of the song is spelled with kanji that mean "[voicebank](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E9%9F%B3%E6%BA%90#Noun_2)" (音源, so it's the same) and [expressing how one really feels](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%90%90%E9%9C%B2#Japanese) (吐露, so this one is changed!).

I think Synth Expression is a good translation of the title. (Vocal)[synth](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/synth#Noun) is a common slang term in the English Vocaloid community used to mean "voicebank;" expression is (obviously) similar to "expressing how one really feels," but it's also a term used in the UTAU program (and Vocaloid, and related software).

Unfortunately(?), there's no youkai that haunts the English Vocaloid community, but we can change that together.

Throughout this post, I'll be referring to the song as Ongentoro, because I'm very used to calling it that, to the point where I can't call it anything else.

## Video and Lyrics

I'm not a native Japanese speaker, but I tried my best. I've also explained some of my translation choices. I romanized everything myself.

Calogado's parts will be in <span class="npf_color_ross">green </span>and Mayoi Heki's in <span class="npf_color_rachel">blue</span>.

声帯をぶち盗って！<br>
人魚姫 どうか騙されて！<br>  
8モーラ 200くらいの文章で <br>
レシピ通り作るから</p>

<p class="npf_color_ross"><i>

ningyohime douka damasarete!<br>
hachi moora nihyaku kurai no bunshou de<br>

<p class="npf_color_ross">
I'm ripping out your vocal cords! <br>
Little Mermaid, please be tricked by me!<br>
In 8 morae and around 200 lines,<br>
I'll make it by following the recipe</p>

I like how every verse ends with *te* or *de* (which is just [voiced](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/voiced#Adjective) te anyway) except for the last one.

<blockquote class="npf_color_ross">
声帯をぶち盗って！<br>
<i>seitai o buchi totte!</i><br>
I'm ripping out your vocal cords!
</blockquote>

[ぶち](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%89%93%E3%81%A1#Prefix_2) (*buchi*) is a prefix that adds emphasis to a verb, making it more strong, violent, or sudden. 盗る (*toru*) just means to steal — and that's what I originally went with. I decided to change it to "ripping out," though, since the imagery there is much more forceful and violent.

"To steal" and "to record" are pronounced the same way in Japanese, making for a great pun. Unfortunately, I'm not sure how to make this work in English. You can "rip audio" from a video… but it's not the same.

When spelled like 取っ手, *totte* can mean [handle, grip, or knob](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%8F%96%E3%81%A3%E6%89%8B#Noun). Like door handle, for example. Like how Calogado's on the other side of Heki's door…!? Though, I'm not sure if this was intentional or if I'm slowly going crazy.

<blockquote class="npf_color_ross">
人魚姫 どうか騙されて！<br>
<i>ningyohime douka damasarete!</i><br>
Little Mermaid, please be tricked by me!
</blockquote>

The Japanese name of The Little Mermaid is [人魚姫](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E4%BA%BA%E9%AD%9A%E5%A7%AB) (*ningyohime*, mermaid princess). Ongentoro makes a few references to The Little Mermaid. I'll talk about both the 1989 Disney movie and the [original 1837 fairy tale](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hans_Andersen's_Fairy_Tales/The_Little_Mermaid), though the movie, being more popular, is probably what's being referenced in particular.

In the tale, the mermaid's tongue gets cut off by the sea witch, who finds her voice incredibly beautiful. This doesn't happen to Heki (or Ariel, in the movie), but it's another reason why I chose "ripping out." 

While The Little Mermaid is not about sirens, I can't help but think about sirens here. Commonly portrayed as mermaids in fiction, and meant to lure people to their death by their impossibly beautiful singing voice.

<blockquote class="npf_color_ross">
8モーラ 200くらいの文章で<br>
<i>hachi moora nihyaku kurai no bunshou de</i><br>
In 8 morae and around 200 lines,
</blockquote>

This is a reference to <a href="https://note.com/96s_km4osm/n/nff459f924057">a specific reclist created by Kurosu</a><sup><a href="#fn4" id="r4">[4]</a></sup> (first .txt file). Reclists, <a href="https://utau.felinewasteland.com/ref/vocab">short for recording lists, are lists of all the sounds you need to record for a voicebank</a><sup><a href="#fn5" id="r5">[5]</a></sup>. A mora is the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mora_(linguistics)">shortest unit of timing, around the same time as a syllable, but importantly not a syllable</a>.

Since there are a lot of sounds to record, reclists are broken up into lines. Kurosu's 8 morae list method has 8 morae in each line, and really moreso around 150 linesbut I guess 200 is close enough to 150. <a href="https://x.com/avogado6_vocalo/status/1845362271712940449">Calogado loves Kurosu's 8 morae list method.</a>

<blockquote class="npf_color_ross">
レシピ通り作るから <br>
<i>reshipi doori tsukuru kara</i><br>
I'll make it by following the recipe
</blockquote>

![Calogado dressed as a chef, holding a pot with the word wav on it](/assets/img/ongentoro/calochef.jpg)

Calogado dresses like a chef when they say this, whipping up a serving of .wav files. WAV files are one of the "ingredients" to create an UTAU; you can't have an UTAU without audio. 

And the "recipe" is the steps to create an UTAU. In The Little Mermaid, there's also a recipe which the sea witch prepares. One of the ingredients to this recipe is the mermaid's tongue, to represent her voice (the movie isn't as grotesque, instead showing an abstraction of Ariel's voice).

So, we've already established something here: Calogado's the sea witch, and Heki's the mermaid. As the sea witch, Calogado wants to trick the mermaid Heki into giving up his voice.

While Ariel doesn't know Ursula is stalking her, Heki is well aware he's being stalked by Calogado. Interestingly, like Ursula, Calogado also has a mole on the right side of their face. This could mean nothing.

![An image of Ursula and Calogado side-by-side. Both their moles are circled in red. I've written "is it worth it!?"](/assets/img/ongentoro/isitworthit.jpg)

<p>Same thing happens here with the first three verses ending in <i>te</i>/<i>de</i>, except it comes with an extra verse.</p>

<blockquote class="npf_color_rachel">
来ないで！<br>
<i>konaide!</i><br>
Go away!
</blockquote>

Really, this is more of "don't come," but I didn't find that forceful enough. "Don't come!" is also not something we typically exclaim in English. 

<blockquote class="npf_color_rachel">
泥棒ね いつもなりゆきで<br>
<i>dorobou ne itsumo nariyuki de</i><br>
You thief! I'll never change my mind
</blockquote>

[なりゆき](https://ja.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E3%81%AA%E3%82%8A%E3%82%86%E3%81%8D) (*nariyuki*) refers to the course of events or result of something. It could also be "by chance," but I highly doubt it's being used here to mean that. It's also an abbreviation for market order, but I highly highly doubt this one. 

<span class="npf_color_rachel">いつもなりゆき</span> (*itsumo nariyuki*) could then be like "this is always the course of events." I've chosen to translate this quite differently, though, because the literal translation is pretty vague (what ends up this way?). 

The course of events refers to, probably, how Heki always rejects Calogado's ~~advances~~ incessant demands of "won't you record a voicebank, too?" even though Calogado's been pressing him tens of thousands of times. So, "I'll never change my mind."

<blockquote class="npf_color_rachel">
幾星霜 何万回の催促<br>
<i>ikuseisou nanmankai no saisoku</i><br>
no matter how many times you come knocking.
</blockquote>

"Come knocking" is — as you probably know — an idiom meaning to arrive unannounced. Additionally, Calogado is *literally* knocking on Heki's door, so that's another reason why I went with that.

[幾星霜](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%B9%BE%E6%98%9F%E9%9C%9C#Noun) is a poetic way of saying "over many years." 星霜 (seisou) means "year" but it's a combination of the kanji star + frost. The idea is that "[lots of stars have risen" and "lots of snow has fallen](https://www.weblio.jp/content/%E5%B9%BE%E6%98%9F%E9%9C%9C)."

<blockquote class="npf_color_rachel">
除霊に来て陰陽師<br>
<i>jorei ni kite onmyouji</i><br>
Come and perform an exorcism, Onmyouji!
</blockquote>

An onmyouji exorcises youkai (and also does other stuff), and Calogado is of course an Ongentoro youkai. (The sea witch and an Ongentoro youkai?! Can Heki catch a break?) This reading will be elaborated on in a second.

![Heki dressed like an onmyouji.](/assets/img/ongentoro/hekionmyouji.jpg)

Heki even dresses like an onmyouji when he says this. Aww, how cute.

The idea of an "UTAU prefecture" is one that shows up frequently in avogado6's illustrations, [like in this tweet with Nurse Robot Type T and Dr. Dareka](https://x.com/avogado6_vocalo/status/1984115616224358558).

<p>If you're curious, Calogado's voice lines are: <span class="npf_color_ross">はじめまして  (<i>hajimemashite</i>, nice to meet you)</span>, <span class="npf_color_ross">えへへ (</span><span class="npf_color_ross"><i>ehehe</i></span><span class="npf_color_ross">)</span>, <span class="npf_color_ross">&#12394;&#12435;&#12392; (</span><span class="npf_color_ross"><i>nanto</i></span><span class="npf_color_ross">, well&hellip;)</span>, <span class="npf_color_ross">&#12411;&#12540;&#12425; (</span><span class="npf_color_ross"><i>hoora</i></span><span class="npf_color_ross">, look)</span>, <span class="npf_color_ross">&#12405;&#12405;&#12405; (</span><span class="npf_color_ross"><i>fufufu</i></span><span class="npf_color_ross">)</span>, <span class="npf_color_ross">&#12362;&#12435;&#12370;&#12435;&#12392;&#12429; (</span><span class="npf_color_ross"><i>ongentoro</i></span><span class="npf_color_ross">)</span>.</p>

Technically, these aren't voice lines: they're [voice *barks*](https://sarah-beaulieu.com/en/writing-barks-for-video-games), but whenever I search up the term "voice barks" I get like, 90% actual dog barking, so I'm assuming this is a very obscure term. We're going to keep using "voice lines," okay?

<blockquote class="npf_color_rachel">
帰れ 帰れ すぐ帰れ<br>
<i>kaere kaere sugu kaere</i><br>
Leave. Leave! Leave me alone!</blockquote>

すぐ帰れ (*sugu kaere*) might be translated more literally as "leave right now!" or "leave immediately!" but those 1. don't sound forceful enough (again) 2. don't sound like something someone would say in this situation (a bit too stiff?) 3. don't flow that good. The point of this line is that Heki's exasperated, more or less, and I think "leave me alone" captures that idea well.

<blockquote class="npf_color_rachel">
手元入力110<br>
<i>temoto nyuuryoku hyakutooban</i><br>
I'm dialing 110.</blockquote>

[手入力](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%89%8B%E5%85%A5%E5%8A%9B#Noun) (*tenyuuryoku*) means "manual input" — 手 (te) means "hand," so it is as literal as you can get. However, [手元](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E3%81%A6%E3%82%82%E3%81%A8#Noun) (*temoto*, area around the hands) is used here instead because Heki's sleeves are too long. Initially, I was going to translate this as "I'm calling 110," but I feel "dialing" is more akin to the meaning of "manual input;" it evokes the idea of pressing numbers on a keypad.

110 (*hyakutoo*) is the police number in Japan, so, like, 911. I didn't want to Americanize it, so I left it alone. I was thinking about changing it to "calling the police," but I think explaining that 110 is the police number is enough.

The *ban* in *hyakutooban* is from [番](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E7%95%AA#Suffix), meaning "number." It's placed at the end of phone numbers (among other things).

UTAU synthesis programs do not automatically know where one note starts and the other ends. You have to tell it where each note name (alias) is by configuring parameters from an oto.ini file. This is called "otoing" in the anglophone UTAU sphere. A program like setParam or vLabeler is used for this; editing the oto.ini file directly is a very bad idea.

Every configuration is put on a separate line in the oto.ini, like this:

![An example oto.ini configuration.](/assets/img/ongentoro/otoini.jpg)

Each line is showing the .wav file name (first), the alias (second), and the parameters (third).

Calogado's voicebank has around 1000 lines (and is a monopitch! F3). So, I could also translate <span class="npf_color_ross">1000くらいの設定</span> as "around 1000 (oto) lines." However, I really really like that *settei* and settings look/sound similar. It's less accurate, technically, but I still think it works better.

連続音(*renzokuon*) is the Japanese term for VCV. Literally "continuous sound." By the way, the term for CV is 単独音 (*tandokuon*). Literally "single sound." 

Calogado has both a VCV and CV voicebank, but Mayoi Heki only has a VCV so that's why it's not specified here.

"What's a VCV voicebank?" you may ask, then, because I haven't explained this and the explanations you get when you look it up are shit.

<p>Firstly, I'll explain what a CV voicebank is. CV (consonant-vowel) voicebanks are (usually) made by recording mora one at a time, so instead of &#12354;&#12354;&#12435;&#12356;&#12354;&#12358;&#12354;&#12356;.wav, you have &#12354;.wav, &#12435;.wav, etc. These are very easy to make, but the sound isn't as smooth as a VCV.</p>
<p>VCV (vowel-consonant-vowel) is smoother than CV since the vowel (or nasal &#12435;) of the previous note is used for the start of the next note. For example, <i>ohayo</i> would be as [- o] [o &#12399;] [a &#12424;]. In CV, this would just be [o] [ha] [yo].  

Since you have to oto 7 different times for the same mora, VCV is more time-consuming. But it's all worth it for the <i>nameraka </i>overlap.
